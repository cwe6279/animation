Configuration reference

Every knob on the talking face, and which file it lives in

Talker is a projected character that listens, thinks, and talks back: a mascot at a park gate, a historical figure in a museum gallery, a storyteller in a library. This page lists what you can change, where, and what we measured when choosing the defaults.

Overview

What runs where

One turn passes through six stages. Local stages cost CPU on the box running the face; cloud stages cost the same on a Raspberry Pi as on a desktop.

localMicrophone capture, 16 kHz mono
local or cloudSpeech to text
--stt
cloud or localBrain, streamed
--llm
cloud or localVoice, sentence by sentence
--tts
localLip sync from word timings
localFace render, 30 or 60 fps
optional, cloudScene notes from the camera
--camera

The face starts speaking the first sentence while the brain is still writing the rest. Every turn prints one [turn] line in the terminal: time from when you stopped talking to the transcript, to the first token, to the first audio. That line is the number to tune against.

Hardware

What to plug in

Tested with a Logitech C920 webcam, a Samson Go Mic USB condenser and a Jabra Speak 510 speakerphone on a Fedora desktop; a Raspberry Pi 5 is the intended kiosk box.

SetupNeedsNotes
Desktop, developingAny USB mic and speaker, optional webcamLocal Whisper is fine. A speakerphone hears itself, so keep half-duplex, the default.
Doorstep or room propProjector or display, a separate directional mic and speaker, optional webcam, internetSeparate mic and speaker are what make --barge-in possible: mic pointed at the visitors, speaker behind or beside it facing away. Aim for a level of 500 to 5,000 on --mic-test at visitor distance.
Raspberry Pi 5 kioskPi 5 with 4 GB or more, USB mic and speaker or a USB audio interface, HDMI projector, camera, internet--profile pi: recognition and everything heavy run in the cloud. Local Whisper takes seconds per turn on a Pi. Pi 4 runs at about half speed. Keyboard-free Wi-Fi setup is on the roadmap.

Placement. Camera at the visitors' eye level, wide enough for a small group and whatever they hold up; keep the projected face out of its view or it may describe itself. Mic within about a metre of where people stand. A cardioid mic rejects sound from directly behind it, so put the speaker on the mic's back side, a metre or more away, facing the visitors past the mic; a windscreen or small baffle helps outdoors but does not replace that placement. A non-USB mic needs a class-compliant USB audio interface (XLR: Scarlett Solo or Behringer UM2 with phantom power for condensers; 3.5 mm plug-in-power mics: an adapter that supplies it). Speaker volume moderate: a loud speaker makes the mic hear the character, which defeats barge-in and can cause false wake-ups.

Calibrating a new setup, the recommended first step. python voice_loop.py --calibrate --mic-device gomic --output-device jabra measures the room, plays the character through the speaker and measures the bleed into the mic, then asks you to talk from the visitor spot. It says whether barge-in is viable, recommends --barge-in-boost, flags mic gain that is too low or too hot, and saves the results to calibration.json on that machine. From then on the loop uses those devices and that boost as defaults whenever the flags are not given.

Measuring speaker bleed by hand. python voice_loop.py --mic-test --mic-device gomic --output-device jabra --play "Testing one two three" plays the character's voice three times through the speaker while printing the mic level. Lines tagged speaker are what the mic hears from the speaker; lines tagged mic are the room and you. Rearrange until the speaker number sits well below your own speaking level, then set --barge-in-boost so the threshold lands between them.

Barge-in check. Run with --barge-in --debug. A barge-in needs half a second of continuous speech (--barge-in-ms) louder than the usual onset threshold times --barge-in-boost (2.5) while the character talks, and that does not follow the rhythm of what the speaker is playing: an echo guard compares the mic's loudness pattern with the outgoing audio and rejects matches ([echo] lines in the log). A USB speakerphone's own mic, such as the Jabra's, cancels its speaker in hardware and is worth trying when mic and speaker cannot be separated. If [barge-in] lines still appear while it speaks and nobody is talking, raise the boost to 4, move or angle the mic, lower the volume, or go back to half-duplex.

Commands voice_loop.py · speak.py

Starting it

Activate the virtualenv first (source .venv/bin/activate). The conversation loop is voice_loop.py; speak.py only speaks text you give it.

# Desktop: local Whisper, Claude Haiku, ElevenLabs v3, the cat's own voice and persona
python voice_loop.py --face cat --mic-device gomic --output-device jabra --debug

# Raspberry Pi: everything heavy in the cloud, fullscreen, 30 fps
python voice_loop.py --face cat --profile pi --mic-device gomic --output-device jabra

# No mic: type in the window, replies still go through the brain
python voice_loop.py --face eve --text-only

# Just say a line (supports [tags])
python speak.py --face skull --text "[whispers] Who dares enter?"

Recommended stacks

  • Desktop, fastest with a good voice: Whisper + Claude Haiku + ElevenLabs flash, or --tts piper to drop the voice's network round trip. About 1.0 to 1.1 s from transcript to first audio.
  • Desktop, everything local, no keys: Whisper + --llm ollama --model qwen3:8b + --tts piper. About 0.75 s to first audio, the fastest pair measured, with a plainer voice and a smaller brain. Choose a standard-attention model so the prompt cache holds.
  • Desktop, best performance: Claude Haiku (Opus for the writing) + ElevenLabs v3. Tags are performed, eyes and voice agree, about 1.7 s to first audio.
  • Pi 5 kiosk: --profile pi (Scribe realtime + Claude Haiku) with --tts piper for an instant offline voice, or ElevenLabs flash when the voice matters more. A local brain on the Pi is untested and would be several seconds per reply; leave it in the cloud.

Control page http://<box>:8001 · --web-port · --no-web

Tune and test it from a phone

Every run serves a small page on port 8001, standard library only and off the audio and render paths, so a kiosk that starts at power-up can still be inspected and tuned from any device on the same network.

TabWhat is there
StatusFace, ears, brain, voice; live mic level; speaking, thinking or dormant; the last [turn] timing line; what was heard and said; vision counts and the latest scene note; recent events.
TuneLive settings with their help text and the flag that sets each at startup: silence gate, barge-in with its threshold and boost, echo guard, idle timeout, engaged, debug overlay, vision interval and change filter. Applied at once, kept until exit.
Test setupMic level meter with the target range, speak a line through the speaker, send a line as a visitor, interrupt, a camera snapshot, and the calibration routine (room, speaker bleed, a person) with its verdict, saved like --calibrate.
ReferenceEvery --flag with its help, default and the value in use this run; the face.json fields of this face with what they do.
Wi-FiNetwork devices, a scan, and join a network through NetworkManager, as on Raspberry Pi OS. The box must be reachable first; a setup hotspot is on the roadmap.

Power-up on a Pi. docs/talker.service is a systemd unit that starts the character with the page at boot: copy it to /etc/systemd/system/, edit the user, path and flags, then sudo systemctl enable --now talker.

Your own flat eye art can be made live too: python tools/unwrap_eye.py faces/<name>/eye_right.png faces/<name>/eye finds the pupil, unwraps the iris, cuts a pupil map in the pupil's real shape, derives lids from the outline and keeps reflections as an overlay, then prints the block to paste into face.json. Any design from the Uncanny Eyes project works as-is: copy its convert/<eye> parts into faces/<name>/eye/ and add a textured_eye block to face.json. The pupil and iris then move on saccades, dilate with emotion (surprise wide, anger a hairline), and the upper lid follows a downward gaze; our squish and tilt still apply on top.

Most-tweaked fields: character.md (personality), voices.elevenlabs (voice id), glow_color, gaze.amount, and the eye and mouth cx/cy positions.
Adding a character faces/_template/ → faces/<name>/

A new face is a folder with a manifest and optional PNGs

  1. Copy faces/_template to faces/<name> and edit face.json. Everything except the manifest is optional; missing art falls back to glowing procedural shapes.
  2. Design on an 800 by 800 canvas and export each feature as a full-canvas PNG with transparency: face_base.png, eye_left.png, eye_right.png, nose.png, and up to six mouths mouth_sil, mouth_pp, mouth_aa, mouth_oo, mouth_ee, mouth_ah. Art is cropped to its opaque bounds at load, so full canvases cost nothing at runtime.
  3. Set the persona and voice in the manifest, then run python speak.py --face <name> --debug --text "Hello" to check placement.

EVE's art is generated by a script rather than drawn: edit the numbers at the top of faces/eve/generate_art.py and rerun it to reshape the head, visor, or eyes. To have an image model draw a face instead, hand it docs/ART_SPEC.md: it fixes the canvas, the layer set, the file names, and the mouth shapes so the output drops straight into a face folder.

Prompt and tags talker/brains/system_prompt.md

One shared prompt, one persona line per face

Two files are concatenated on every turn. The shared talker/brains/system_prompt.md (everything below its "System Prompt" heading) holds delivery rules: the performance-tag vocabulary, tags before the words they colour, most sentences untagged, one to three spoken sentences, no markdown. The face's character.md holds only personality: who they are, traits, tone, boundaries. It is appended as "Character: …", so editing a face never touches the shared rules and vice versa.

How tags reach the voice and the eyes

Tags are the ElevenLabs v3 vocabulary: emotional states, reactions, tone cues, character cues. The voice performs them only on ElevenLabs v3; Flash and edge get them stripped so nothing reads "sigh" aloud. Separately, tags with an emotional meaning move the eyes:

EyesTags that trigger it
happy, smiling squinthappy, excited, cheerfully, playfully, laughs, giggle, chuckle, light chuckle, sigh of relief, warmly, proud, teasing
angry, narrowed and furrowedangry, frustrated, furious, shouting, growls, menacing
annoyed, mild narrowannoyed, irritated, sarcastically, whiny, deadpan, bored, exasperated, smug
sad, droopedsad, sorrowful, tired, regretful, resigned tone, hesitant, sigh, quietly, softly, weary
surprise, wide opensurprised, gasps, awe, amazed, shocked, curious, scared, nervous, confused
neutralcalm, flatly, matter-of-fact, whispers, serious, thoughtful

Accents, roles and beats like [pauses] affect only the voice. The map is TAG_TO_EMOTION in talker/phoneme_scheduler.py; add words there to teach the eyes new tags.

If the face feels busy: every model we tested tags more than the prompt asks, about two per reply. Tighten rule 3 in the prompt. For a longer persona, a historical figure's biography, speech habits, and the facts it should stick to, the character string can be as long as you like; the shared prompt handles delivery, the persona handles who is speaking.

Audio devices voice_loop.py flags

Pick devices by name, not number

PortAudio renumbers devices whenever something is plugged in, so name fragments are safer: --mic-device gomic --output-device jabra. Matching is case-insensitive and prefers the PipeWire entry over the raw hardware one.

python voice_loop.py --list-devices                     # every input and output with its native rate
python voice_loop.py --mic-test --mic-device gomic      # levels and live transcripts, nothing else
python voice_loop.py --mic-test --mic-device gomic --record recordings/   # also save clips for the benchmark
  • Levels. Aim for 500 to 5,000 while talking on the mic test's scale; peaks above about 28,000 clip. Adjust gain in the system sound settings.
  • Rates. Devices open at whatever they support and are resampled, so a 48 kHz speakerphone plays 24 kHz speech and a 48 kHz mic feeds a 16 kHz recognizer. --mic-rate forces the mic's open rate.
  • Silence. --silence-ms sets how long a pause ends your turn. Default 600 for local recognizers, 500 for cloud. Lower feels snappier and cuts more sentences.
  • Echo. The mic is ignored while the face speaks. --barge-in keeps listening and interrupts when you talk, which needs headphones or a mic that cannot hear the speaker.
Wake mode voice_loop.py --wake · face.json wake_words

Wait to be called by name

--wake starts the character engaged and, after the idle timeout or a goodbye, drops it to dormant: it listens, and keeps watching if vision is on, but answers nothing until a wake word is heard. --start-dormant makes it wait to be called from the start, which is what a kiosk wants and what the Pi profile does. The wake words are the face's wake_words list, else its name; --wake-word "eve, hey eve" overrides. List likely mis-hearings in the face's wake words ("marsha", "martha", "marcia"); fuzzy matching is used only for longer names so "coat" cannot wake a goat. Words after the name in the same sentence are answered at once; the name alone gets a reply to being called.

It goes dormant again after --idle-timeout seconds of silence counted from the end of its own last reply (60 by default), when the brain decides the conversation is over (it ends a farewell with the marker [end], which the loop removes before the voice sees it), or when a visitor says a sleep word on its own: "stop", "wait", "hold on", "hang on", "pause", "quiet", "go to sleep", "enough", "goodbye" and a few more, up to four words. A sleep word also cuts the character off mid-sentence, and with --barge-in it works while it is still talking. --sleep-word "..." or sleep_words in face.json override the list. The Pi profile turns wake mode on.

Vision voice_loop.py --camera · talker/vision.py

Eyes for the character, off unless you switch them on

Pass --camera c920 (a name fragment or an index; --list-cameras shows them) and a watcher takes three frames about 0.3 s apart every 9 seconds, shrinks them, and asks a fast vision model what changed since its last look, given the previous one-line state, so the brain receives a short delta such as "a child came in holding a red balloon" rather than a repeat description. A burst is also taken the moment a visitor starts talking, so the note is fresh when the transcript lands, and a visual question waits up to 2.5 s for it. When a burst changes the scene, the delta enters the conversation as an inner observation, "(You notice: ...)", ahead of the next thing a visitor says, and the character is told it is its own eyesight, not text to recite: react in your own words only if it matters, never repeat the observation. Quiet turns add nothing and the visitor's words are never altered.

SettingEffect
--cameraTurns vision on. Without it nothing runs and no camera opens. --no-vision forces it off.
--vision-interval, --vision-framesSeconds between bursts (9) and frames per burst (3).
--vision-modelClaude Haiku 4.5 by default: about a sixth of a cent per described burst.
--vision-changeThe change filter: a burst goes to the model only when its thumbnail differs from the last described one by more than this mean pixel change (default 0.035, i.e. 3.5%; a still room is about 1%, a raised object 4 to 8%, a person entering 10% or more), plus a forced refresh every 90 s. An empty room costs nothing.

Privacy. Frames are discarded the moment they are described; only text notes are kept, and only the most recent few. If the model flags an emergency, someone hurt or in distress, fire or a clear hazard, that one burst and its note are saved under emergencies/<timestamp>/, which is gitignored, and the brain is told to stay calm, keep it short, and ask for an adult.

Prove it before you trust it

pytest tests                                  # 37 tests, no mic or display needed
python tools/check_face.py faces/<name>             # validate a face folder against the art spec
python tools/bench_stt.py --synth                   # recognizers on clean and noisy synthesized clips
python tools/bench_stt.py recordings/               # the same on your own recorded clips
python tools/bench_llm.py                           # brains: speed and how well they follow the tag spec

For your own clips, record with --mic-test --record recordings/, then correct the draft lines in recordings/transcripts.txt before running the benchmark.

Troubleshooting

When it misbehaves

SymptomLikely cause and fix
It speaks "Sorry, I could not think of an answer"The brain call failed. The terminal has an [error] LLM failed line: usually a missing or unscoped key, or no credits.
Coughs or noises come out as wordsWhisper runs behind a voice-activity filter and a confidence cut-off and ignores clips under 0.35 s. If it still happens, raise --silence-ms a little or move the mic closer to where people speak.
Mic never hears anythingWrong device or gain. Run --mic-test and check the level line; pick the device by name.
Every turn is exactly 15 seconds longRoom noise above the endpointer's threshold. Fixed in the current build; if it recurs, lower the mic gain.
"Invalid sample rate" or "no default output device"Old builds. Devices now open at any supported rate and fall back to another output.
Voice "does not exist" on ElevenLabsAdd the community voice to My Voices, and give the key the voices permission.
Lips early or late--sync-offset in 0.02 s steps with the debug overlay on.
Face reads "sigh" out loudOnly ElevenLabs v3 performs tags; every other voice strips them. Check --tts-model.
PyAudio will not installNeeds the Python headers and PortAudio: dnf install python3-devel portaudio-devel, then pip install pyaudio.
